# Emailing a prospective supervisor: style guide

Built 2026-11-07 from 10 university and faculty pages (links at the bottom). Re-run with `/outreach ... refresh style`.

## What most sources agree on

- **Read first.** Read their page and recent papers before writing. Queen's says at least 2 to 3 of the latest publications [1]. Tulane says at least one recent paper [6]. Also [2][3][10].
- **Check their page for rules, and follow them.** Alberta's graduate school says to follow any instructions a supervisor gives prospective students [2]. Don't ask what the page already answers [1][10].
- **Keep it short.** Under 250 words at Queen's [1], about 200 in [3], one screen at Tulane [6], and short at BU [7]. Our 110 to 170 is safely inside all of these.
- **Mention specific work, in your own words.** Name the paper and say something specific about it [1][6][7][9][10]. Don't copy from their abstract or website [1][9].
- **Write to one person.** Generic or mass email is the most common complaint across these pages [1][2][5][6][9][10].
- **Use a clear, descriptive subject line** [1][2][5][10]. Queen's suggests the form "Prospective {Masters/PhD} student interested in [topic]" [1].
- **Say what you want plainly.** State who you are and what you're asking [1][3]. Alberta's graduate school says to request a conversation about fit rather than ask for a position outright [2], and Queen's warns against demands [1].
- **Use their correct title and name.** "Dear Professor Smith", and check that you have the right person and institution [1].
- **Follow up once, then stop.** See the timing note below [1][2][6]. Repeat emails don't help [10].

## Things professors say annoy them

- Mass email, or anything that reads like it [2][5][6]
- Generic text that describes you but not their work [9][10]
- Emails that only say a paper was read or interesting, with nothing specific [10]
- Flattery and self-praise [6]
- Listing grades, test scores and awards, or asking about admission chances [6][9]
- Listing every job and project, unless relevant [9]. Link or attach the CV rather than repeating it [9].
- Questions already answered on their website [1][10]
- Long, unfocused messages [1][6]
- Fancy formatting [6]. Plain text is more likely to get a reply [5].
- AI-written email with no real personal effort. UVA's page names LLM-generated messages directly [5], and Queen's says not to use AI without personalising the result [1]. Read and edit every draft before sending it.

## Where sources disagree

- **Attach a CV?** Most say yes, as a PDF [1][3][7][10]. Tulane says to send a link instead of attachments [6], and UCSC prefers a link [9]. Attach it by default, and follow the professor's own page if it says otherwise.
- **Follow-up timing.** About a week at Tulane [6], about 2 weeks at Queen's [1], 2 to 3 weeks in Alberta's graduate guide [2]. University pages lean longer than 7 to 10 days.
- **Email first or apply first?** Several US CS professors want the application first, or no email at all [4][7][8]. Some differ by degree: Cornell's Ellis asks MS students to email directly but tells PhD applicants it isn't needed [8]. Always check the individual page.
- **Asking about taking students.** Reasonable where the page doesn't say [3]. Don't ask it if the page already says [10].

## Not found in these sources

These came from the original brief and no source read here states them. They are kept as house rules, not as sourced findings:

- "Dear Sir/Madam" as a named annoyance. The nearest sourced rule is to use the correct title and name [1].
- "Do you have funding?" as a first line. Sources say little on funding. One treats it as an optional question [3], and Queen's notes that supervisors may not have immediate funding [1].
